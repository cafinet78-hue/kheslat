In 1814, Londoners walked an elephant across the River Thames. No bridge. No boat. Just ice.
That February, London got so cold the river froze from bank to bank. So Londoners did the sensible thing. They threw a party on it.
First came the skaters. Then tents selling gin. Then swings, skittles, and printing presses that printed your name on a card for a few pence.
Someone roasted a whole sheep on the ice, charged sixpence just to watch it, then sold it at a shilling a slice.
And then, just below Blackfriars Bridge, came the elephant.
Four days later, the thaw arrived. The ice split, and tents, printing presses and about forty barges went sailing down the river.
Luckily, the elephant was already on the other side.
Unluckily, the printing presses were not.
But the real reason the river froze was a bridge. Old London Bridge stood on nineteen narrow arches, and it worked almost like a dam. Above it, the water barely moved.
And still water freezes much faster than moving water. It's why a puddle freezes overnight, while the stream right next to it keeps on running.
In 1831, London opened a new bridge with just five wide arches, and pulled the old one down. The river sped up. And the Thames has not frozen over in London since.
So the greatest party ever held on the Thames wasn't stopped by the weather.
It was stopped by a bridge.
